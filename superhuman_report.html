Superhuman — March 2026
What Superhuman Taught The World

“How would you feel if you could no longer use Superhuman?” — In 2017, Rahul Vohra took a simple question from Sean Ellis and turned it into the most widely adopted product-market fit framework in startup history. The methodology spread virally through Y Combinator, became required reading for early-stage founders, and spawned dozens of SaaS tools for running “the PMF survey.”

The Signal They Invented (And Stopped Reading)

Superhuman built one of the most powerful tools for reading behavioral signals in startup history. Then the industry reduced it to a metric. And the company itself stopped applying its own methodology — rebranding a 40M-user product based on narrative instead of signal, measuring email velocity instead of dependency, and assembling four acquisitions into a suite without asking the question that made them famous.

The Method: Loss Signal, Not Satisfaction Survey

Sean Ellis created the test around 2009 after analyzing 100+ startups. The question — “How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?” — is not a satisfaction survey. It’s a loss signal.

The difference is critical. A satisfaction survey asks “do you like this?” — it measures attitude (stated preference). The Ellis question asks “what would you lose?” — it measures dependency through loss framing (revealed dependency). It activates loss aversion — the tendency to react more strongly to loss than to gain (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). Ellis himself deliberately avoided the word “satisfaction” because he believed “a good manager is never satisfied.”

Vohra’s innovation was threefold: segmentation (isolate the High-Expectation Customer — for Superhuman, “Nicole,” a busy executive handling 100-200 emails daily), correction (50% roadmap on doubling what the “very disappointed” love, 50% on removing barriers for the “somewhat disappointed”), and cycling (quarterly measurement, PMF as OKR).

Result: 22% → 32% → 48% → 58% in 18 months. Among the highest publicly disclosed PMF scores ever achieved.

58% PMF. >90% retention. >120% NRR. 60%+ growth. And yet — exit below peak valuation. The metric predicted product loyalty. The metric did not predict business viability. Reason: constrained addressable market. $30-40/month for email when Gmail is free.

How The Industry Broke The Framework

The First Round Review article became one of the most-read startup posts of the decade. And almost everyone took the wrong lesson from it.

What they took: “40% is the magic number. Run the survey. If you hit 40%, you have PMF.”

What the framework actually says: The 40% threshold is a diagnostic starting point, not a destination. Ellis himself: “I don’t think it’s that firm. To me, the real power is having some kind of target for the team to be shooting for.” The power was in the system — segment, correct, cycle. Not in the number.

The result: “Running a PMF survey” became a checkbox. Buffer ran the survey on an ultra-engaged group and got 78% “very disappointed” — but broader retention was poor. The purpose of the survey “was misunderstood for almost 9 years” — the disappointment data is better used as a filtering lens to understand how users interpret the product, not as a pass/fail grade.

This is like taking a thermometer, noting that 36.6°C means healthy, and treating the number as the goal instead of the diagnostic tool.

Five Signals The Company Isn’t Reading

“Superhuman is vague, corporate, sounds like an AI chatbot, and most importantly, means absolutely nothing.”

- Tech press reaction to the Grammarly → Superhuman rebrand, October 2025

Grammarly — a brand with 15 years of recognition, 40M daily active users, $700M ARR — was renamed Superhuman. If the company had applied its own framework: “How would you feel if you could no longer use Grammarly?” Ask the 40M users. Measure the dependency on the old name before destroying it.

“You rapacious information and identity thieves better get ready for me to go full McConaughey on you.”

- Kara Swisher, after discovering Grammarly’s “Expert Review” used her identity without consent, March 2026

A company whose philosophy is “AI that amplifies people” built a feature that literally replaces people with AI imitations — without consent. Stephen King, Kara Swisher, Julia Angwin became AI “experts” unknowingly. Class action lawsuit filed. Feature disabled within days. This isn’t a bug. It’s a behavioral signal: the company shifted from reading what users need to building what looks impressive.

“Is this really a good metric to aim for? Don’t we want productivity tooling to result in less email, not more?”

- Hacker News commenter, on Superhuman’s claim that users send 72% more emails per hour

The company that invented measurement of dependency now measures velocity. “72% more emails per hour” is a volume metric, not an impact metric. Another HN user: “The Superhuman users I’ve worked with start skimming emails and sending super-short replies.”

“This reminds me exactly of Dropbox’s transformation from simple, reliable file storage into bloated software.”

- Hacker News commenter, on the Grammarly-Superhuman merger

Four products from different companies — Grammarly (2009), Coda (2014), Superhuman Mail (2014), Rows (2026) — assembled under one name in 15 months. Meanwhile: Microsoft launches 365 E7 ($99/user/month with Copilot bundled, May 2026). Google includes Gemini in Workspace for free. The two platforms that own the email infrastructure are embedding AI directly. An assembled suite competing against native platforms.

“The undisclosed acquisition price implies a down-round exit at best, and a fire sale at worst.”

- Industry analyst, on Superhuman Mail’s acquisition by Grammarly at estimated $300-500M vs. $825M peak valuation

Standalone email tools don’t survive. Postbox — acquired and shut down (2024). Skiff — acquired by Notion, shut down (2024). Salesforce Engage for Gmail — retired (2024). Superhuman Mail — acquired below peak valuation (2025). The pattern: email is a feature of a platform, not a platform itself.

The Question Nobody Is Asking

58% “very disappointed” was measured for Superhuman Mail — a focused email client for busy executives handling 100-200 emails daily.

That product no longer exists in the form it was measured.

Today, “Superhuman” is Grammarly + Coda + Mail + Go + Rows. Different product. Different CEO. Different brand. Different audience — from “Nicole” with 200 emails to Grammarly’s 40M users.

If you asked the same question today — “How would you feel if you could no longer use Superhuman?” — who exactly answers? The Grammarly user who doesn’t understand why their writing tool is now named after an email app? The Coda user whose Coda Brain was shut down for Superhuman Go? The Rows user whose product closes in May?

PMF is not a number. It’s a relationship. And relationships don’t transfer through acquisition.
