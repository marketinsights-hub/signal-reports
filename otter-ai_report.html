The Pattern

The Quiet Takeover

Otter built its base as a passive transcription tool. In the push to $100M ARR, it crossed a line users never drew—from recording meetings to joining them, from transcribing to emailing summaries to the whole room, from note-taker to autonomous agent.

A federal class-action lawsuit, “malware” comparisons across platforms, and mass account deletions aren’t responses to a bad update. They’re responses to a relationship that changed without consent.

Where Otter Sits

Zoom, Microsoft, and Google are all embedding meeting AI natively. Competitors retreat into features. Otter alone advances into autonomous agents. But users who chose a standalone tool may have chosen it because they wanted something they could control.

What This Signals

The AI meeting space is splitting into tools (do what you tell them) and agents (act on your behalf). Most users don’t know they need to choose yet.

The companies that let users choose how much autonomy to grant—a clear permission layer for every action—will capture the trust premium this generation of AI meeting products is burning.
